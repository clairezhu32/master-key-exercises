How to Launch a Side Project in 90 Days While Working Full Time

The hard part is not building. It is deciding what counts as done, resisting the urge to polish, and getting the thing in front of real people before your motivation runs out. Here is the three-phase quarter that ends in a launch.

Why 90 days is the right constraint

Side projects rarely die from difficulty. They die from having no deadline. With unlimited time every decision can be deferred and every feature can be added, until the scope expands past the point where finishing is possible. A hard 90-day window forces subtraction: with twelve weeks and maybe six to ten usable hours a week, you have to ask what the smallest version is that a real person could use.

It also matches your motivation curve. Enthusiasm for a new idea reliably lasts a few weeks and then drops around week five. A quarter is short enough that the finish line is visible before that dip arrives. Twelve months is not.

The mistake: building for weeks before talking to anyone

The dominant failure mode is building in private. It feels productive — the code, the designs and the brand are the visible work, and talking to strangers is uncomfortable. So people spend eight weeks building, launch to silence, and conclude nobody wants it.

Usually the idea was not the problem. Nobody confirmed the problem existed, in that form, for those people, before a hundred hours went into a solution. Ten conversations in week one would have reshaped everything that followed.

Building in private also hides a comfortable form of procrastination. Choosing a framework, redesigning the logo, refactoring, setting up analytics for a product with no users — all of it feels like work and none of it risks hearing "no thanks." Perfectionism is fear wearing a productive costume, and it grows scope every time you imagine launching.

The reframe: your goal for the quarter is not a finished product. It is evidence — proof that a specific group of people will do a specific thing. Pick the smallest build that produces it.

The three-phase framework

Roughly a third of the quarter each. The distribution matters as much as the work.

Weeks 1–4

Validate and scope

Talk to ten people in the audience, define one user and one job, then cut the build to fit the remaining weeks.

Weeks 5–8

Build the smallest version

Ship a working thing end to end, ugly but complete, and get it in front of five real users while you build.

Weeks 9–12

Launch and learn

Set a public date, launch to real channels, measure behaviour, then decide to continue, change or stop.

Weeks 1–4: validate, then cut ruthlessly

Write the idea as one sentence: who it is for, what job it does, how you will know it worked. If you cannot name the user specifically — not "small businesses" but "freelance designers who invoice fewer than ten clients a month" — you are not ready to build. Then have ten conversations with people in that group. Ask what they currently do about the problem and what they have already tried. You are looking for improvisation — a spreadsheet, a workaround, a paid tool they dislike — because that is the strongest buying signal there is.

Weeks 5–8: build end to end, ugly on purpose

Build the whole path a user takes at low quality before improving any part of it. A rough complete flow teaches you more than one beautifully finished screen. Aim for something usable by the end of week six, then spend weeks seven and eight fixing only what blocks a user. Work in weekly shippable slices — a fortnight of invisible refactoring is where enthusiasm goes to die. Get five real people using it while you build, and watch what they do rather than asking whether they like it.

Weeks 9–12: launch, then read the evidence

Launch in week nine or ten, not twelve — you want a fortnight of real data before the quarter closes. Post where your users already gather and message everyone you interviewed. Define success before the numbers arrive: one behavioural metric, one qualitative signal. Then make an honest call — continue, change direction, or stop. Stopping with real evidence is a successful quarter; drifting for six more months is not.

What to do in week one

No building. Five things, all doable in a few evenings.

  • Write the one-sentence version: for [specific person], this helps them [specific job], and I will know it worked if [observable behaviour].
  • Book your hours. Put the actual weekly slots in the calendar for all twelve weeks. Six real hours beat a theoretical fifteen.
  • List twenty people in your audience and message ten asking for fifteen minutes.
  • Do three of those conversations this week. Ask about their current workaround, not about your idea.
  • Write the exclusion list — everything you are consciously not building in v1. Accounts, integrations, mobile app, custom design: name them and let them go.

Then pick the smallest launch surface: a single page, a form, a manual service behind a nice front end. Most projects need far less machinery than the founder assumes.

How to handle setbacks and stalls

Around week five the excitement fades, a technical problem eats a whole session, and a busy fortnight removes your evenings. Every finished project went through this.

Protect the streak, shrink the session. On a bad week do twenty minutes rather than nothing. The point is keeping the project loaded in your head; a two-week gap costs more than the hours missed.

Cut scope, never the date. When you fall behind the instinct is to push the launch — resist it, because a moved date moves again. Remove features instead.

Distinguish a stall from a signal. A technical block is a stall: time-box it, take the ugliest workaround, move on. Five interviews saying the problem is not painful is a signal, and deserves a change of direction.

Review every two weeks: what shipped, what is blocked, what gets cut. Daily self-assessment is anxiety with a spreadsheet.

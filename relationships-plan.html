How to Make Friends in a New City: A 90-Day Plan

Moving somewhere new does not make you bad at friendship — it removes the repetition that built every friendship you already have. Here is how to rebuild that repetition deliberately, in one quarter, without relying on luck or extroversion.

Why 90 days is the right timeline for new friendships

Friendship runs on a slower clock than we expect. Research on how acquaintances become friends points to something like forty to sixty hours of shared time before someone counts as a casual friend. Meet for coffee monthly and you are looking at years. See the same person twice a week at a climbing session and you cross that threshold in a couple of months.

That arithmetic is the whole strategy. Ninety days is twelve weeks of recurring contact — enough for two or three relationships to move from "person I recognise" to "person I text." It also converts a vague ache into a project with actions in it, which is what loneliness takes away.

The mistake: collecting events instead of building repetition

Newly arrived people usually try hard in the least effective way. They go to a different meetup every week, attend one-off networking nights, join three apps, say yes to everything. After a month they are exhausted, have met fifty people, and are no closer to a friend than on arrival.

A one-off event produces a pleasant conversation and no second encounter. Friendship needs proximity, repetition and unplanned time together — and a rotating calendar of new events supplies only the first. Ten different rooms once each is far weaker than one room ten times.

The second mistake is waiting for reciprocity. You make a genuine connection, enjoy it, then wait to be invited. Both sides wait, nothing happens, and both quietly conclude the other was not interested. In a new city somebody has to do the inviting more often than feels fair, and for a few months that person is you. It is temporary, and it is not a character flaw.

The reframe: stop trying to meet more people. Pick two or three recurring places and become a regular. Repetition, not volume, is what turns strangers into friends.

The three-phase framework

Each phase has one job: create exposure, convert it, then deepen it.

Weeks 1–4

Recurring exposure

Choose two or three weekly activities with the same people and attend every time. Attendance is the only metric.

Weeks 5–8

Invitations

Move familiar faces off-site. Small, specific, low-stakes invitations — coffee after, a walk, a film.

Weeks 9–12

Depth and rhythm

Turn the two or three that clicked into something with its own cadence, and introduce people to each other.

Weeks 1–4: build the rooms you keep returning to

Pick two or three activities that meet on a schedule with a stable group: a class, a team, a choir, a run club, a volunteer shift, a regular game night. Two criteria matter more than the subject — it repeats weekly, and the same people come back. A monthly event fails the first test; a huge drop-in fails the second. For four weeks the only goal is attendance. Learn names and use them, aim for one slightly-longer-than-necessary conversation each time, and say yes to everything.

Weeks 5–8: make the invitations

By week five familiar faces exist. Now convert them. The formula is specific, small and easy to decline: "A few of us are getting food after Thursday — want to come?" Vague openness ("we should hang out sometime") rarely converts, because it puts the planning burden on someone who barely knows you. Aim for one or two invitations a week and count invitations extended, not acceptances. Expect half to fall through for ordinary reasons, and re-invite once — that follow-up is where most friendships are made or dropped.

Weeks 9–12: deepen and connect

Two or three people should now stand out. Shift from one-off invitations to something with rhythm — a standing Tuesday run, a monthly dinner. Then do the thing most people skip: introduce your new people to each other. Groups are stickier than pairs, and the person who convenes a group becomes central to it fast.

What to do in week one

Five actions. None require being an extrovert.

  • Choose two recurring activities that meet weekly with the same people, and sign up for both. Not three one-off events.
  • Calendar every session for the full twelve weeks, so attending is a default rather than a nightly decision.
  • Attend one this week and learn three names. That is the entire success criterion.
  • Start a names file — name, where you met, one detail. Referring to that detail next time is the highest-return social move available.
  • Reconnect one dormant tie: anyone you know loosely in the area, plus a friend elsewhere who might know someone here.

Also decide your realistic social budget — two evenings a week, or one. Overcommitting in week one and burning out by week four is how this plan usually fails.

How to handle rejection and the flat weeks

There will be an evening you leave feeling invisible, an unanswered message, a plan cancelled twice. Those moments do not mean what they feel like they mean.

Assume logistics before rejection. Most non-replies are about busy lives, not judgement. Invite once more, then move on gracefully. Reading silence as a verdict on your worth stops you inviting anyone, which is the only real failure mode.

Score inputs, not outcomes: sessions attended, conversations started, invitations extended. You cannot control whether someone has capacity for a new friend right now.

Expect week six to feel flat. The novelty of a new place fades before the friendships arrive, and this dip is where most people quietly stop going. The hours still accumulate even when a week feels unremarkable.

Never miss two sessions in a row. Skipping twice is how you become someone the group no longer expects, and being expected is the asset you are building.
